# Perspective outline: AI agents in neuroscience publishing

## Working title

Writing neuroscience for humans and machines: authorship, agents, and accountability

## Author

Ebrahim Haroon (Abe)

## Core question

Who are we writing for when AI agents search, summarize, draft, and synthesize the literature—and how should those agents be named, disclosed, and held accountable?

## Sections

### 1. Introduction

- Papers remain human documents; machines increasingly process them at scale.
- Neuroscience spans disciplines, species, and methods, making synthesis especially hard.
- LLMs summarize well but misattribute methods, species, and causal claims.

### 2. The naming problem

- Tools vs. agents: when is an LLM a cited instrument versus a named collaborator?
- Why listing an AI system as an author fails tests of responsibility and accountability.
- Transparency alternatives: in-text citation, methods disclosure, supplementary logs.

### 3. Author-verified, machine-readable summaries

- Proposal: structured summaries alongside traditional prose.
- LLM first pass; human verification before publication.
- Precedents: STAR Methods, genomics metadata standards, structured reporting.

### 4. Implications for neuroscience

- Cross-species and cross-method integration depends on consistent extraction.
- Bias toward paradigmatic studies when machines re-read prose repeatedly.
- Opportunity to honor diverse model systems through better structured representation.

### 5. A path forward

- Design for humans and machines simultaneously.
- Require disclosure, verification, and auditable agent configurations.
- Build open tooling that makes provenance inspectable.
